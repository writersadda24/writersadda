# How to run a focused documentation audit

A documentation audit does not need to begin with a large spreadsheet or a complete rewrite. Start with a bounded audience and task, then inspect the material that helps readers complete it. The result should be a prioritized set of actions, not a pile of comments.

## 1. Choose the reader journey

Pick one audience and a small group of related tasks: for example, a new developer making a first API call, or an administrator configuring a product. A focused scope makes it possible to review the content in context and reach useful findings quickly.

Agree on what the audit should answer. Are users failing to get started? Are engineers spending time answering repeat questions? Is content difficult to maintain? The question influences what evidence to collect.

## 2. Gather evidence

Review the relevant pages and follow the documented steps where access permits. Talk with support, product, or engineering colleagues about recurring questions. If available, examine search terms, feedback, support tickets, and product analytics. Treat these signals as clues, not proof by themselves: a page with few views may be hard to find, or it may simply serve a smaller audience.

Record each finding with a page or task, the observed issue, its likely reader impact, and the evidence behind it. Separate confirmed product defects from documentation problems and questions that need an owner to resolve.

## 3. Look for common failure patterns

- Important tasks are missing or difficult to discover.
- Instructions skip prerequisites or expected results.
- Examples do not match the current product behavior.
- Names, concepts, or UI labels vary across pages.
- Content repeats and has no clear source of truth.
- Error guidance describes symptoms but gives no next step.
- Readers cannot tell whether content applies to their version or role.

## 4. Prioritize by reader impact and effort

Address high-impact blockers first: incorrect or unsafe instructions, broken onboarding paths, and gaps that prevent users from completing core tasks. Group small consistency fixes where they can be resolved together. Track larger structural work separately so that the first improvements are not delayed by a full redesign.

For each action, assign an owner, a reviewer, and a way to confirm the fix. A useful backlog item is specific enough that someone can act on it without repeating the audit.

## 5. Share a useful result

A concise audit deliverable can include:

- Scope, audience, and tasks reviewed
- Evidence sources and access limitations
- Highest-priority findings with examples
- Recommended actions and suggested owners
- Open questions for product or engineering
- A proposed order for follow-up work

Avoid unsupported claims such as a guaranteed reduction in support volume. Where baseline data exists, agree with the relevant team on how to measure whether changes helped.

## A small audit can create momentum

The goal is not to label every page as good or bad. It is to find the points where information blocks a real task, make the next action clear, and give the team a realistic way to improve the content over time.
